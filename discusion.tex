\subsection{Physical Determinants of Device Placement Reliability}

The measurements indicate that UWB tag placement should not be considered to be interchangeable between body locations. BS on the 6.5 GHz band is not only significant signal attenuation, but also delay estimation bias that changes the empirical distribution of the ranging error.

The tag placement resulted in relatively small ranging errors for most body locations (in terms of LOS), but the best placement was determined by the scenario. In the indoor corridor, the lowest MAE was obtained at the wrist (6.42 cm), then the hip (7.19 cm) and head (10.43 cm). In the outdoor region, the chest and head placements showed the lowest LOS errors with 4.62 cm and 4.87 cm for MAE and head placement, respectively. The LOS results illustrate that when the body does not block the direct path, we can make other placements to get centimeter-level ranging performance. When BS is introduced, the placement hierarchy of the placements is different: the head is the most robust indoor NLOS placement with MAE $\approx$ 18.66 cm, whereas the hip takes MAE $\approx$ 97.76 cm. As an industrial safety and tracking device, this result is in favor of helmet-mounted or high shoulder placement over belt-mounted placement when precision is required, but still recognizes ergonomic challenges. This is consistent with the report by Otim et al. who found that the head (forehead) is a good placement and reported errors of $\approx$ 13 to $\approx$ 20 cm in propagation conditions, mainly due to the possibility of LOS. The wavelength is approximately 4.6 cm at 6.5 GHz and can be used for creeping wave diffraction around the curved shape of the human skull. Being of dimension around 20 cm, the skull has a curvature radius and its diffraction at such a frequency is important. This propagation mechanism, also discussed by Otim et al., is why the head placement appears relatively stable in the measurements.

The larger deterioration seen for torso placements in the chest and hip areas is consistent with a stronger physical obstruction of the direct path. The torso is in effect a lossy dielectric obstacle and not a piece of random noise. When the torso produces BS, the receiver is usually relying on delayed reflected paths and hence the ToF estimation bias and therefore the ranging error is greater. Attenuation is certainly part of it, but the main observable effect in this work is a positive ranging bias that often adds delays of 0.2 to 0.5 m because of tissue transmission, as reported by Tian et al. and Otim. The nearly full-meter NLOS error difference between head and hip placements indicates that waist-belt tags are not accurate enough to ensure accuracy without bias mitigation. For safety-critical tracking, torso-mounted tags should be placed with compensation technologies or alternative placement methods.

\subsection{The Constructive Multipath Phenomenon}

One of the counterintuitive patterns we observe for our measurements is the constructive multipath effect in the indoor corridor. Indoor environments are known to be worse in positioning - walls, floor, and ceiling all play a role in multipath fading and interference. This is supported by other studies where NLOS propagation and multipath interference have a negative impact on the ranging accuracy.

For body-worn tags in this data, the LOS and NLOS results are more nuanced. In LOS, the error samples are mostly gathered closer to low-error values, with 84.43% of the samples falling below 0.2 m and a global gamma distribution being the best fit. In NLOS/BS, only 34.23% of the samples are below 0.2 m and the error structure becomes more heavy-tailed log-normal, and the indoor-outdoor comparison becomes a function of placement. A Mann-Whitney U comparison between outdoor and indoor NLOS measurements for the same extremities showed that for the head, hand, wrist, knee, and ankle, the MAE ranging error was consistently and significantly \textit{lower} indoors than outdoors. The hand placement, for example, had a large effect size ($r = -0.693$, $p \ll 0.001$) and was located in the narrow corridor. This is a strong indication of the narrow corridor environment.

The physical reason behind this pattern is constructive multipath. In an open outdoor field, when BS impacts the direct LOS path, there are few alternative paths, so the receiver may estimate distance from weak delayed components or fail to track robustly. The outdoor NLOS results in Section \ref{sec:results} show this loss of ranging ability: measurements get degraded and eventually cease to be recorded beyond approximately 6 m for torso placements, namely chest and hip. The lack of nearby reflective surfaces reduces the availability of compensatory paths. In the indoor corridor, however, the surrounding geometry can provide reflected components that maintain enough received energy for ToF estimation. Even for the best placement, like the wrist, outdoor NLOS MAE increases to 24.07 cm compared to 22 cm indoors, which is consistent with the indoor advantage in this measurement campaign.

Welch et al. noted that the body can severely attenuate signals up to 23.6 dB in light-multipath conditions and up to 6.8 dB in dense-multipath ones. Indoor multipath is usually considered a degradation source because it can produce false first paths. In this paper, a placement-dependent qualification is added. As Muqaibel et al. also show, narrow corridors can be lossy waveguide structures that convey energy and provide alternative propagation paths, such as that observed in body shadowing measurements of Pradabphon et al. and Schmitt et al. For extremities like hand or wrist, which move more easily than torso, these paths can reduce the ToF bias compared to outdoor BS without any nearby reflectors.

\subsection{Failure of Gaussian Assumptions in Parametric Models}

A key statistical result of this study is that the 6.5 GHz BS ranging errors are poorly represented by zero-mean Gaussian models. As shown in Section \ref{sec:results}, the absolute error distribution changes from a gamma-like distribution in LOS to a heavy-tailed log-normal distribution in NLOS.

This result can explain why standard positioning algorithms (e.g., Extended Kalman Filters and Least Squares multilateration) have difficulty with correcting biases in indoor positioning systems affected by BS. Traditional state estimation filters are generally based on zero-mean Gaussian noise ($\mathcal{N}(0, R)$) as their measurement update system. The log-normal distribution for NLOS absolute errors is not a case of zero-mean Gaussian noise, and hence has a positive skewness and a systematic bias. If an EKF takes this heavy-tailed, non-zero-mean error as Gaussian noise, it can underestimate the uncertainty at the range and eventually move to a displaced position. G\"uvenc et al. also show that NLOS conditions introduce systematic biases that degrade standard least-squares-based positioning solutions. These results provide an empirical reference to the design of UWB positioning systems used in human-centric tracking. As mentioned by \cite{Mirama2021}, NLOS handling and non-Gaussian error modeling are still open challenges for pedestrian localization systems. In future systems, distribution-aware observation models should be adopted. The practical direction is to detect the NLOS at the start, and then to move the measurement model from a LOS-based gamma or Gaussian approximation to a heavy-tailed log-normal bias-compensation model.
