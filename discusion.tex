\subsection{Physical Determinants of Device Placement Reliability}

The measurements indicate that UWB tag placement should not be treated as interchangeable across body locations. For the 6.5 GHz band, BS is observed not only as signal attenuation but also as a delay-estimation bias that changes the empirical distribution of the ranging error.

Under LOS conditions, tag placement produced relatively small ranging errors for most body locations, although the best-performing placement depended on the scenario. In the indoor corridor, the lowest MAE was obtained at the wrist (6.42 cm), followed by the hip (7.19 cm) and the head (10.43 cm). In the outdoor scenario, chest and head placements showed the lowest LOS errors, with MAE values of 4.62 cm and 4.87 cm, respectively. These LOS results indicate that, when the body does not block the direct path, several placements can provide centimeter-level ranging performance. Once BS is introduced, the placement hierarchy changes: the head becomes the most robust indoor NLOS placement, with MAE $\approx$ 18.66 cm, whereas the hip reaches MAE $\approx$ 97.76 cm. For industrial safety and tracking applications, this result supports helmet-mounted or high-shoulder placements over belt-mounted alternatives when precision is required, while still recognizing ergonomic constraints. This tendency is consistent with Otim \textit{et al.} \cite{ref4}, who identified the head (forehead) as a favorable placement and reported errors of approximately 13 to 20 cm across propagation conditions, partly due to a higher probability of LOS. At 6.5 GHz, the wavelength is approximately 4.6 cm, which can support creeping-wave diffraction around the curved geometry of the human skull. With a physical dimension of approximately 20 cm, the skull provides a curvature radius that can make diffraction relevant at this frequency. This propagation mechanism, also discussed by Otim \textit{et al.} \cite{Otim2019}, helps explain why the head placement remains comparatively stable in the measurements.

The larger deterioration observed for torso placements, mainly the chest and hip, is consistent with stronger physical obstruction of the direct path. In these cases, the torso behaves as a lossy dielectric obstacle rather than a simple source of random noise. When the torso produces BS, the receiver tends to rely on delayed reflected paths, increasing ToF estimation bias and therefore ranging error. Attenuation contributes to this effect, but the dominant observable outcome in this work is a positive ranging bias, frequently adding delays of 0.2 to 0.5 m due to tissue transmission, consistent with observations reported by Tian \textit{et al.} \cite{ref16} and Otim \textit{et al.} \cite{ref4}. The nearly full-meter NLOS error difference between head and hip placements suggests that standard waist-belt tags may not satisfy precision requirements without explicit bias mitigation. For safety-critical tracking, torso-mounted tags should therefore be paired with compensation methods or alternative placement strategies.

\subsection{The Constructive Multipath Phenomenon}

One counterintuitive pattern in the measurements is the \textit{Constructive Multipath} effect observed in the indoor corridor scenario. Indoor environments are often associated with poorer positioning performance because walls, floor, and ceiling can introduce multipath fading and interference. This expectation is supported by studies in which NLOS propagation and multipath interference degrade ranging accuracy \cite{WANG2025101979}.

In this dataset, the LOS and NLOS results show a more nuanced behavior for body-worn tags. Under LOS, the error samples remain mostly concentrated near low-error values, with 84.43\% of the samples below 0.2 m and a global gamma distribution providing the best fit. Under NLOS/BS, only 34.23\% of the samples remain below 0.2 m, the error structure shifts toward a heavy-tailed log-normal distribution, and the indoor-outdoor comparison becomes placement-dependent. A Mann-Whitney U comparison between outdoor and indoor NLOS measurements for the same extremities showed that, for the head, hand, wrist, knee, and ankle, the MAE ranging error was consistently and significantly \textit{lower} indoors than outdoors. For example, the hand placement exhibited a large effect size ($r = -0.693$, $p \ll 0.001$), favoring the confined corridor environment.

The physical mechanism behind this pattern is constructive multipath. In an open outdoor field, when BS affects the direct LOS path, there are few alternative paths, so the receiver may estimate distance from weak delayed components or fail to track robustly. The outdoor NLOS results in Section \ref{sec:results} show this loss of ranging capability: measurements become degraded and eventually cease to be recorded beyond approximately 6 m for torso placements, namely chest and hip. The absence of nearby reflective surfaces reduces the availability of compensatory paths. In the indoor corridor, by contrast, the surrounding geometry can provide reflected components that maintain enough received energy for ToF estimation. Even for a favorable placement such as the wrist, outdoor NLOS MAE increases to 24.07 cm, compared with 22 cm indoors, which is consistent with the observed indoor advantage in this measurement campaign.

Welch \textit{et al.} \cite{ref15} noted that the body can attenuate signals severely, up to 23.6 dB in light-multipath conditions and reduced to 6.8 dB in dense-multipath environments. Indoor multipath is usually treated as a degradation source because it can create ``false first paths''. The present results add a placement-dependent qualification to that view. As documented by Muqaibel \textit{et al.} \cite{Muqaibel2006}, narrow corridors can behave as lossy waveguide structures that channel energy and provide alternative propagation paths; related behavior is also observable in the body-shadowing measurements of Pradabphon \textit{et al.} \cite{Pradabphon2019} and Schmitt \textit{et al.} \cite{Schimtt2019}. For extremities such as the hand or wrist, which change spatial orientation more easily than the torso, these reflected paths can reduce effective ToF bias compared with outdoor BS without nearby reflectors.
\subsection{Failure of Gaussian Assumptions in Parametric Models} A key statistical result of this study is that 6.5 GHz BS ranging errors are not well represented by a zero-mean Gaussian model. As shown in Section \ref{sec:results}, the absolute-error distribution changes from a gamma-like distribution in LOS to a heavy-tailed log-normal distribution in NLOS.

This result helps explain why standard positioning algorithms, including Extended Kalman Filters (EKF) and Least Squares multilateration, can struggle to correct biases in indoor positioning systems affected by BS. Traditional state-estimation filters often formulate their measurement update models under the assumption of zero-mean Gaussian noise ($\mathcal{N}(0, R)$). The log-normal distribution observed for NLOS absolute errors implies positive skewness and a non-zero systematic bias. If an EKF processes this heavy-tailed, non-zero-mean error as Gaussian noise, it can underestimate ranging uncertainty and converge to a displaced position. G\"uvenc \textit{et al.} \cite{Guvenc} further show that NLOS conditions introduce systematic biases that degrade standard least-squares-based positioning solutions.

These findings provide an empirical reference for the design of UWB positioning systems used in human-centric tracking. As reviewed by \cite{Mirama2021}, NLOS handling and non-Gaussian error modeling remain open challenges for pedestrian localization systems. Future systems should therefore include distribution-aware observation models. Based on the present characterization, a practical direction is to detect the onset of NLOS conditions and adapt the measurement model from a LOS-oriented gamma or Gaussian approximation toward a heavy-tailed log-normal bias-compensation model.
