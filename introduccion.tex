Indoor Positioning Systems (IPS) have become part of several human-centered applications, including industrial safety, navigation, and asset tracking. Within these systems, Ultra-Wideband (UWB) is frequently selected because it can provide accurate Time-of-Flight (ToF) ranging under controlled propagation conditions \cite{ref6, ref8}. When the mobile node is worn on the body, that performance can change substantially. One relevant source of degradation is \textit{body shadowing} (BS), which appears when the user's body obstructs or modifies the propagation path between the tag and the anchor \cite{ref16}.

In this study, the tag is the body-worn mobile node and the anchor is the fixed reference node. Figure~\ref{fig:system_model} illustrates the BS condition by contrasting LOS and NLOS links between both nodes. Biological tissue has high permittivity due to its water content, so it can attenuate, reflect, refract, and diffract radio-frequency signals \cite{ref15, ref18}. These propagation changes can introduce ranging errors that reduce system reliability in applications such as industrial safety \cite{Kianfar2020}.

Previous studies have quantified this effect in high-accuracy systems. Under LOS (Line-of-Sight) conditions, reported errors are generally lower and often in the decimetric range, depending on the tag placement and the experimental setup \cite{ref4, ref16, ref18}. Under NLOS (Non-Line-of-Sight) conditions, the degradation is larger: mean absolute errors from 1.2 m to over 2 m have been reported for affected body placements \cite{ref4}, ranging errors exceeding 14 m have been documented at unfavorable body orientations in RF-based systems \cite{Schimtt2019}, and additional studies report attenuations up to 8 dB caused by BS \cite{Ruonan2019}. These results show that BS must be considered explicitly when IPS are designed for body-worn tracking.

Although several mitigation strategies have been explored, much of the available work has focused on the 3-5 GHz frequency range \cite{ref4}. The 6.5 GHz band remains less documented for body-worn UWB ranging and is still relevant for experimental evaluation. In ToF-based ranging, temporal resolution is mainly determined by pulse duration and receiver timing capabilities rather than by wavelength alone; picosecond-scale pulses can enable finer $\Delta t$ estimation, subject to practical electronic constraints. Operation at this band can also increase attenuation in biological tissues, which makes the BS effect important to measure directly. For this reason, the present study focuses on ranging error under BS and on the probability distributions that describe the measured absolute error. In the collected data, LOS errors remained mostly in the centimeter-to-decimeter range, while BS-induced NLOS conditions increased the MAE up to 97.76 cm and produced maximum errors of 2.9 m for unfavorable torso placements.


The objective of this article is to characterize the impact of BS on 1D range estimation for UWB systems operating at 6.5 GHz. This work extends preliminary research \cite{danny_daniel_and_victor_quintero} through a larger body-placement analysis and a scenario-aware statistical treatment. The main contributions are:

\textbf{Body Placement Analysis:} Seven on-body tag placements are compared under the same measurement protocol: head, chest, hip, wrist, hand, ankle, and knee. The resulting MAE, standard deviation, and maximum-error values are used to derive a placement hierarchy for body-worn tracking applications.

\textbf{Statistical Ranging Error Modeling:} Goodness-of-fit criteria and non-parametric tests are used to compare positive-support models for absolute ranging error. The analysis shows a transition from gamma-like LOS errors to heavy-tailed log-normal NLOS errors, which is relevant for filters that rely on Gaussian observation assumptions.

\textbf{6.5 GHz Indoor/Outdoor Characterization:} The measurements compare an indoor corridor and an outdoor open field at 6.5 GHz. The results document cases in which reflected indoor components partially preserve ranging observability for limb-mounted tags under BS.

The rest of the article is structured as follows: Section \ref{sec:methodology} details the experimental methodology, including the hardware configuration, experimental scenarios, data collection protocol, and statistical analysis procedure. Section \ref{sec:results} presents the statistical characterization of the ranging error by tag placement and channel condition. In Section \ref{sec:discussion}, the interpretation of the findings and their practical implications are discussed. Finally, Section \ref{sec:conclusions} summarizes the key conclusions and proposes directions for future research.
