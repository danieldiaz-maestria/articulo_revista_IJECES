We included probabilistic analysis to relate the measured errors to observation models commonly used by localization algorithms. Since the metric we study is absolute ranging error ($e_i \geq 0$), the most popular distributions that are positive support are gamma, log-normal, and exponential. Because traditional filters like the Kalman Filter implicitly assume that the measurement noise is Gaussian, we considered the Gaussian assumption as a reference hypothesis and not as the expected distribution of the final model for absolute error. The AIC and BIC were calculated for the body placement subset of 84,511 samples, excluding the baseline TAG measurements.

Table \ref{tab:aic_bic} shows the distribution fits by scenario and channel condition: indoor LOS, indoor NLOS, outdoor LOS, and outdoor NLOS. In each group, the seven body placements are combined. This grouping avoids mixing propagation environments while preserving enough samples for distribution fitting. Placement-dependent behavior is analyzed separately through Table \ref{tab:resumen_indoor} and the non-parametric contrasts. This combined view is consistent with prior body-shadowing studies that report both channel-condition behavior and body-placement-specific effects \cite{ref4, Otim2019}.

For the LOS data, the AIC/BIC comparison selects the gamma family in both propagation scenarios. The fitted parameters were:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \text{Indoor LOS: }  $\alpha=0.5026, \theta=0.1840~\mathrm{m},\quad \mathrm{loc}=0.0000~\mathrm{m}.$
    \item \text{Outdoor LOS: } $ \alpha=0.9480,\quad \theta=0.1237~\mathrm{m},\quad \mathrm{loc}=0.0000~\mathrm{m}.$
\end{itemize}

This result is also visible in Figure \ref{fig:ajuste_los}: the LOS error samples are bounded at zero and keep a right-skewed shape, which a gamma model represents more naturally than a symmetric Gaussian curve.

For the NLOS data, the ranking changes: the log-normal model gives the lowest AIC/BIC values in both scenarios. The estimated parameters were:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \text{Indoor NLOS: } $\mu=-0.9186,\quad \sigma=0.8016,\quad \mathrm{scale}=0.3991~\mathrm{m},\quad \mathrm{loc}=-0.0427~\mathrm{m}$
    \item \text{Outdoor NLOS: }  $\mu=-1.0749,\quad \sigma=0.5173,\quad \mathrm{scale}=0.3413~\mathrm{m},\quad \mathrm{loc}=0.0372~\mathrm{m}$.
\end{itemize}


The pooled NLOS histogram in Figure \ref{fig:ajuste_nlos} explains this selection: compared with LOS, the distribution has a heavier right tail caused by larger positive ranging errors under BS.

The same change in both scenarios suggests that the gamma-to-log-normal shift is not only an artifact of pooling indoor and outdoor measurements.

In the NLOS Q-Q plot, the largest departure from the normal reference line is found in the upper quantiles. These points correspond to high positive error samples and match the right tail of the fitted NLOS model.

The Shapiro-Wilk test gave $p \ll 0.001$ for LOS and NLOS samples.

\subsection{Non-Parametric Statistical Testing}

The placement contrasts were handled by non-parametric tests. Levene's test gave $p \ll 0.001$, which means that the group variances were not equal and ANOVA was not used for this comparison. Mann-Whitney U was then used to compare configurations and locations. Comparing the best-performing placements (Head/Wrist) with the worst-performing placements (Hip/Chest) produced a large effect size ($r = 0.968$ in NLOS, $p \ll 0.001$), demonstrating that tag placement is a key factor in the reliability of a 6.5 GHz UWB ranging system.
